Cooking glossary

289 kitchen words and what they mean, in English and Ukrainian.

289 terms

Reading recipes20
Active time
Minutes when the cook must actually work, rather than wait for rising, chilling, or baking.
Batch
A quantity prepared together under the same recipe and conditions, such as one tray of biscuits.
Consistency
How thick, thin, smooth, or firm a mixture is; for example, pancake batter should pour but not look like water.
Cooking cue
A visible, audible, or tactile sign that tells you when to proceed, such as softened onions or bubbling sauce.
Cooking time
Time when the food is being cooked, heated, or otherwise processed as the recipe specifies.
Doneness
The point to which food has cooked. Appearance alone may not prove that meat or poultry is safe.
Food-safe temperature
A verified internal temperature used with relevant cooking guidance to reduce the risk from harmful microbes.
Ingredient list
The foods and other edible components required for a recipe, usually shown with quantities before the steps.
Optional ingredient
A component the recipe can work without, though omitting it may change flavor or appearance.
Passive time
Time when a recipe progresses mostly without hands-on work, such as a two-hour dough rise.
Preparation time
Time spent getting ingredients and equipment ready before the main cooking begins.
Ratio
The relationship between quantities, such as two parts water to one part grain.
Recipe
A set of ingredients, amounts, and instructions for making a dish. A good recipe also states the yield and important timing.
Recipe scaling
Changing recipe quantities to make more or fewer portions while keeping sensible ingredient proportions.
Resting period
A specified pause that allows a mixture or cooked food to settle; it is not always part of the heating time.
Serving
The amount of a dish intended for one person on one occasion; it may differ from a nutrition-label serving size.
Substitution
Using another ingredient in place of the original; flavor, texture, and cooking behavior may change.
To taste
An instruction to adjust seasoning according to preference, usually a little at a time.
Total time
Elapsed time from starting a recipe until it is ready; it can include both active work and waiting.
Yield
How much finished food a recipe produces, such as six portions or one kilogram of dough.
Recipe cues and expressions23
Clean skewer test
A skewer inserted into some cakes comes out without wet batter; melted chocolate or moist crumbs can affect this cue.
Coats the back of a spoon
A sauce is thick enough to leave an even film on a spoon instead of running off immediately.
Dash
An informal instruction for a small addition, often of a liquid; the amount is not standardized.
Evenly coated
The coating, oil, sauce, or seasoning covers the food without obvious bare patches.
Fork-tender
Soft enough that a fork enters with little resistance, often used for potatoes or slowly cooked meat.
Generously seasoned
Contains a noticeable amount of seasoning, but the exact amount still needs tasting and judgment.
Golden brown
A warm brown surface color used as a visual cue for many baked or fried foods; it does not guarantee safe internal cooking.
Heaped spoonful
A spoon filled above its rim; its amount depends strongly on the ingredient and spoon.
Just set
Firm enough to hold its shape but still delicate; often said of custard or egg dishes.
Level spoonful
A spoon filled to a flat top, usually measured by sweeping away the excess.
Lightly packed
Pressed gently into a measuring cup without strong compression; often used for brown sugar.
Lukewarm
Slightly warm rather than hot; a useful description for some liquids used with yeast.
No longer pink
A common visual description for some cooked meats, but color alone is not a reliable food-safety test.
Pinch
A very small amount held between fingers; for repeatability, weighing is more precise.
Room temperature
The temperature of the surrounding room; in baking, this often describes ingredients that are not cold from the refrigerator.
Shimmering oil
Oil that moves and gleams on a heated pan; it is hot, but should not necessarily be smoking.
Slightly underdone
A dish is intentionally stopped before the final desired doneness because it will finish cooking later.
Soft peak
A tip formed by whipped cream or egg whites that bends over when the whisk is lifted.
Stiff peak
A whipped mixture forms an upright point that holds its shape when the whisk is lifted.
Translucent
Letting some light through. Cooked onions often turn translucent before they become brown.
Until fragrant
Heat an ingredient just until its smell becomes noticeable; spices and garlic can burn if heated too long.
Until reduced by half
Continue heating or processing until about half the original liquid volume remains.
Until tender
Cook until the food yields easily to a fork or bite, depending on the recipe.
Food science and chemistry30
Acidity
A property linked to acids in food; it affects taste, protein behavior, and microbial growth.
Alkalinity
A property associated with basic substances; it can change browning, texture, and some dough reactions.
Colloid
A mixture containing very tiny particles or droplets distributed through another substance without simply dissolving.
Concentration
The amount of a substance present in a given amount of mixture, for example salt in brine.
Crystallization
The formation of organized solid crystals from a liquid or melt, affecting sugar candies and chocolate.
Diffusion
The movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration; salt spreading through a liquid is an example.
Emulsifier
A substance that helps oil and water stay mixed by stabilizing their boundary; egg yolk contains natural emulsifiers.
Emulsion
A mixture in which tiny droplets of one liquid are dispersed through another, such as oil in a salad dressing.
Emulsion stability
How well an emulsion resists separating back into layers over time.
Enzymatic browning
Darkening caused by enzymes reacting after a fruit or vegetable is cut, as with sliced apples.
Enzyme
A protein or other biological catalyst that speeds up specific reactions, such as breaking down starch or proteins.
Foam stability
How long air bubbles remain trapped in a food such as whipped egg whites before collapsing.
Gel network
A network that traps liquid and makes a food hold its shape, as in jelly or a set custard.
Maillard reaction
A set of reactions between amino compounds and sugars during heating that creates brown colors and complex roasted flavors.
Moisture content
The proportion of water inside a food, which influences texture, weight, and storage behavior.
Osmosis
Movement of water through a selectively permeable barrier toward a side with a different dissolved-substance concentration.
Oxidation
Chemical changes involving oxygen or electron transfer that may alter flavor, color, or fat quality.
pH
A scale describing how acidic or basic a substance is; lower values mean greater acidity.
Phase separation
A mixed food separates into distinct parts, such as a layer of oil above a dressing.
Protein coagulation
Proteins join into a more solid network, causing a mixture such as heated egg to become firm.
Protein denaturation
A change in a protein's shape caused by heat, acid, or other conditions; egg white turning opaque is a familiar example.
Saturated solution
A liquid that has dissolved as much of a particular substance as it can at a given temperature.
Solubility
How much of a substance can dissolve in a liquid under given conditions, such as sugar in warm water.
Starch
A carbohydrate stored by plants that can absorb water and change texture when heated, helping thicken soups or sauces.
Supersaturation
A state where a liquid temporarily contains more dissolved material than is normally stable, important in candy making.
Surface tension
A property of a liquid's surface that influences droplet formation and foams.
Suspension
Solid particles dispersed in a liquid that may settle if left undisturbed, such as cocoa in milk.
Syneresis
Liquid being squeezed out of a gel or set food; watery yogurt is a common example.
Viscosity
How strongly a liquid resists flowing; honey is usually more viscous than water.
Water activity
A measure of how available water is for chemical reactions and microbial growth, not simply the total moisture content.
Heat and temperature18
Boiling point
The temperature at which a liquid boils under the current pressure; water boils at a lower temperature at high altitude.
Conduction
Heat moving through direct contact, such as from a hot pan into the bottom of a pancake.
Convection
Heat carried by moving air or liquid, such as circulating hot air in an oven.
Core temperature
Temperature measured near the thickest or coldest relevant part of food; used to judge cooking progress and safety.
Freezing point
The temperature at which a liquid forms a solid; dissolved sugar or salt often lowers it.
Heat capacity
How much energy a material needs to change its temperature; heavy cookware can store substantial heat.
Heat transfer
The movement of thermal energy from something hotter to something cooler.
Hot spot
An area of a pan or oven that heats more strongly than its surroundings, causing uneven cooking.
Melting point
A temperature or range at which a solid becomes liquid, important for fats and chocolate.
Residual heat
Heat retained in a pan or food after the heating source is switched off.
Smoke point
The approximate temperature where a fat begins to release visible smoke; repeated heating and impurities change it.
Surface temperature
How hot the outside of food or cookware is; it may differ greatly from the center.
Temperature gradient
The difference in temperature between locations, such as the surface and center of a thick roast.
Temperature recovery
How quickly cookware, oil, or an oven returns to its target heat after cold food is added or a door is opened.
Thermal equilibrium
A state in which parts of a system reach the same temperature so net heat transfer stops.
Thermal mass
How much heat an object can store and release, helping a heavy pot stay hot after ingredients are added.
Thermal radiation
Energy travelling as electromagnetic waves, such as radiant heat from an oven grill element.
Thermal shock
Damage or stress caused by a rapid temperature change; some glass and ceramic cookware can crack.
Taste, aroma and texture32
Aftertaste
A flavor or sensation that remains after food has been swallowed.
Airiness
A light texture containing many small pockets of air, such as a well-risen sponge cake.
Aroma
Smells released by food that strongly affect how its flavor is perceived.
Aroma intensity
How strong a food's smell seems before and during eating.
Aromatic complexity
The presence of several recognizable aroma notes instead of just one dominant smell.
Astringency
A drying, puckering sensation caused by substances such as tannins in some teas or fruits.
Brittleness
The tendency to snap rather than bend, typical of thin caramel or hard candy.
Chewiness
How much chewing food requires before it can be swallowed; desirable in some breads but not all meats.
Creaminess
A smooth, rich sensation often created by fine particles, fat, or a stable emulsion.
Crispness
A brittle surface that breaks with a light snap, like the edge of a thin cracker.
Crunchiness
A firm structure that makes a noticeable cracking sound when bitten, like raw carrots or nuts.
Density
How compact and heavy a food feels for its size; dense bread usually has fewer large air pockets.
Flakiness
A structure that separates into thin layers or flakes, common in pastry and some fish.
Flavor
The combined perception of taste, smell, and other sensations when eating.
Flavor balance
A pleasing relationship among sweet, sour, salty, bitter, savory, and aromatic elements.
Flavor contrast
A deliberate difference between elements, such as tangy sauce with rich fried food.
Flavor intensity
How strong the overall flavor seems, regardless of whether it is pleasant.
Graininess
A texture with noticeable tiny particles, sometimes desirable in porridge but not in smooth custard.
Grittiness
An unpleasant feeling of hard, sand-like particles while eating.
Gumminess
A sticky, dough-like texture that clings to the teeth, sometimes a sign of underbaked bread.
Juiciness
The sensation of liquid released during biting and chewing.
Mealiness
A dry, crumbly texture with small soft particles, sometimes found in overcooked potatoes or fruit.
Mouthfeel
How a food physically feels in the mouth, including creaminess, dryness, or graininess.
Pungency
A sharp or hot sensation triggered by substances in chili, pepper, mustard, or horseradish; it is not a basic taste.
Rubberiness
An overly tough, springy texture that can result from overcooking some proteins.
Silky texture
Extremely smooth food that glides easily across the tongue, like a well-strained purée.
Sogginess
A wet, soft texture where food was expected to stay crisp, like fries left under a lid.
Springiness
The tendency of food to return toward its original shape after being pressed.
Stringiness
A texture with long strands or fibers, sometimes found in overgrown vegetables or certain meats.
Taste
A sensation detected by taste receptors, such as sweet, salty, sour, bitter, or umami.
Tenderness
How easily food yields under a fork or bite without needing much force.
Umami
A savory basic taste associated with substances such as glutamates; common in broth, mushrooms, and aged cheese.
Bread and baking concepts27
Baker's percentage
A way to express bread ingredients as percentages of total flour weight, which is defined as 100%.
Biological leavening
Gas formed by living microorganisms during fermentation, typically yeast.
Chemical leavening
Gas produced by reactions involving baking soda or baking powder instead of living yeast.
Closed crumb
Bread crumb with small, evenly distributed holes, typical of many sandwich loaves.
Crumb
The inside structure of a baked bread or cake, including the size and distribution of air pockets.
Crust
The outer layer of bread or pastry, usually drier and more browned than the inside.
Dough development
Changes during mixing and resting that help a dough become cohesive and workable.
Dough elasticity
The tendency of stretched dough to pull back toward its original shape.
Dough extensibility
The ability of dough to stretch without tearing, useful for pizza and some flatbreads.
Dough hydration
The weight of water relative to flour, usually expressed as a percentage; 700 g water and 1,000 g flour give 70% hydration.
Dough inclusions
Pieces mixed into dough, such as dried fruit or seeds, that change texture and sometimes hydration.
Dough maturity
A stage when fermentation and structure have developed enough for the next step.
Dough strength
How well dough holds its structure and resists collapse; it depends on flour, hydration, and handling.
Dough temperature
The actual temperature inside dough, which affects fermentation speed and handling.
Enriched dough
Dough containing substantial fat, sugar, eggs, or dairy, often producing softer, richer baked goods.
Gluten
A group of proteins in wheat and related grains; when hydrated and worked, they help give dough stretch and structure.
Gluten network
A connected structure of hydrated wheat proteins that can hold gas as bread dough rises.
Lean dough
Bread dough with little added fat or sugar, such as many simple flour-water-salt loaves.
Leavening
The creation or expansion of gas in dough or batter that makes baked goods lighter.
Open crumb
Bread crumb with large or irregular holes, often associated with some artisan loaves.
Oven spring
The rapid increase in loaf volume early in baking, driven by expanding gases and continued gas production.
Overproofed dough
Dough that has risen too far and may lose strength or collapse during baking.
Preferment
A portion of flour, water, and fermenting culture prepared before the final dough to influence flavor and structure.
Proof readiness
How close dough is to being suitably risen; it is judged by its behavior, not a fixed clock time alone.
Staling
Changes in bread structure over time that make it firmer and less pleasant, even without visible mold.
Starter activity
How vigorously a fermented culture produces bubbles and changes volume, helping assess readiness for baking.
Underproofed dough
Dough baked before sufficient rising, which may produce a tight crumb or unwanted bursting.
Pastry, sweets and frozen desserts14
Aeration level
How much air is held in a mixture, affecting the volume and lightness of mousses or cakes.
Chocolate shine
A smooth reflective appearance associated with a well-formed chocolate surface.
Chocolate snap
The crisp sound and clean break of properly structured solid chocolate.
Cocoa butter crystal forms
Different ways cocoa butter can solidify; the stable form helps chocolate look shiny and snap cleanly.
Custard curdling
The formation of lumps when egg proteins set too quickly or unevenly from excessive heat.
Fat bloom
A grayish coating that appears when cocoa butter crystals change or migrate to the surface.
Freezing point depression
A decrease in freezing temperature caused by dissolved substances such as sugar, affecting ice cream softness.
Ice crystal size
The size of frozen water particles in ice cream; smaller crystals generally feel smoother.
Melting behavior
How quickly and evenly a dessert melts; important in chocolate and frozen desserts.
Overrun
The increase in volume caused by air added during ice-cream freezing; high overrun makes it lighter.
Scoopability
How easily a frozen dessert can be portioned with a scoop at serving temperature.
Sugar bloom
A pale rough coating caused when moisture dissolves surface sugar and it later recrystallizes.
Sugar crystal
A solid, ordered piece of sugar; crystal size affects the smoothness of candies and icing.
Sugar inversion
The splitting of sucrose into glucose and fructose, which changes sweetness and crystallization behavior.
Meat, fish and egg science16
Albumen
The clear liquid surrounding an egg yolk before cooking; it becomes opaque and firm when heated.
Bone-in cut
A meat portion that still contains bone; it may cook differently from a similar boneless portion.
Boneless cut
A meat portion with its bones removed; useful when comparing recipe yields and cooking times.
Collagen
A structural protein in connective tissue that can gradually transform during moist, prolonged cooking, improving tenderness.
Connective tissue
Tissues such as collagen-rich membranes that support muscles and often need long cooking to soften.
Dark meat
Poultry muscle such as thigh or leg that generally contains more myoglobin than breast meat.
Fat cap
A layer of fat on the outside of a meat cut, such as a beef roast.
Lean meat
Meat containing relatively little fat; it can dry out faster under high heat.
Marbling
Fine streaks of fat within muscle that can influence tenderness, juiciness, and flavor.
Meat grain
The direction muscle fibers run through a cut; shorter fibers generally feel easier to chew.
Meat shrinkage
A reduction in size and weight during cooking as proteins tighten and moisture or fat is lost.
Moisture loss
Liquid leaving food during heating or storage, often making cooked protein less juicy.
Muscle fiber
A long cell that makes up animal muscle; fiber direction influences how meat feels when chewed.
Myoglobin
A muscle pigment that contributes to meat's color; color changes do not reliably prove food safety.
Protein setting point
A temperature range where proteins start forming a firmer structure; it varies by food and conditions.
White meat
Paler poultry muscle, typically breast meat, with different fat and cooking characteristics from dark meat.
Fermentation and microorganisms15
Acetic acid
The main acid responsible for vinegar's characteristic sharpness and smell.
Aerobic conditions
Conditions with available oxygen; some microorganisms grow best in them.
Anaerobic conditions
Conditions with little or no oxygen; they favor certain microbes and can pose special food-safety risks.
Beneficial mold
A selected mold used under controlled conditions in certain cheeses and fermented foods; unidentified mold on food is not automatically safe.
Carbon dioxide production
The creation of CO₂ during some reactions or fermentations; the gas forms bubbles and raises dough.
Fermentable sugar
Sugar that a particular microorganism can use, often producing acids, gases, or alcohol.
Fermentation culture
Living microorganisms used to produce a controlled change in food, as in yogurt or bread.
Fermentation rate
How quickly microorganisms change a food; temperature, salt, sugar, and acidity all affect it.
Inoculation
Introducing a chosen microbial culture into food, such as adding yogurt starter to milk.
Lactic acid
An acid produced by many fermenting bacteria that contributes tartness and changes food acidity.
Lactic acid bacteria
A group of bacteria that can produce lactic acid from sugars and are important in many fermented foods.
Microbial growth
An increase in numbers of microbes; in food it can be helpful for fermentation or dangerous when pathogens grow.
Microbial spoilage
Unwanted changes caused by microorganisms that make food unacceptable or potentially unsafe.
Overfermentation
Fermentation that continues beyond the desired stage, causing excessive acidity, gas, or texture changes.
Wild yeast
Naturally occurring yeasts from the environment that may participate in food fermentation.
Food safety and hygiene31
Allergen cross-contact
An unintended transfer of allergenic proteins into food that was not meant to contain them; ordinary cooking does not reliably remove allergens.
Allergen declaration
Information identifying specified allergenic ingredients in food according to applicable labeling laws.
Best-before date
A date usually connected with expected quality under proper storage; its legal meaning depends on the jurisdiction.
Botulism
A severe illness caused by botulinum neurotoxin; unsafe low-acid preservation and certain oxygen-free conditions can create risk.
Cleaning
Removing visible soil, grease, and food residue from a surface; it does not by itself guarantee sufficient germ reduction.
Cold chain
Keeping chilled or frozen food at suitable controlled temperatures during transport, storage, and service.
Contaminant
An unwanted biological, chemical, or physical substance in food, such as bacteria or glass fragments.
Critical control point
A step where control is essential to prevent, eliminate, or reduce a significant food-safety hazard.
Critical limit
A measurable boundary at a critical control point that separates acceptable from unacceptable operation.
Cross-contamination
The transfer of harmful microbes or other contaminants from one food, surface, or hand to another.
Disinfection
A process designed to inactivate many harmful microorganisms on surfaces; it differs from cleaning and may require specific products.
Food allergen
A food protein or other component that can trigger an immune reaction in a person with a food allergy.
Food intolerance
Difficulty tolerating a food without the same immune mechanism as a food allergy, as with some forms of lactose intolerance.
Food safety hazard
Something biological, chemical, or physical that could make food harmful to health.
Food traceability
The ability to follow food and ingredients through production and distribution using records and identifiers.
Food-contact surface
A utensil, board, counter, or machine part that touches food during preparation.
Foodborne illness
Illness caused by eating food containing harmful microbes, toxins, or other hazards.
HACCP
A systematic approach to identifying food-safety hazards and controlling the significant ones at appropriate process steps.
Mycotoxin
A harmful chemical made by certain molds; removing visible mold does not necessarily remove the toxin.
Natural toxin
A harmful substance naturally present in some plants, animals, or microorganisms; cooking does not destroy every toxin.
Pathogen
A microorganism or other biological agent capable of causing disease.
Perishable food
Food that needs prompt use or controlled storage because it can spoil or become unsafe relatively quickly.
Product recall
An organized action to remove food from sale or use after a safety or regulatory problem is found.
Ready-to-eat food
Food intended to be eaten without an additional safety-related cooking step, such as a prepared salad.
Safe handling
Practices for receiving, preparing, cooking, cooling, and storing food that reduce contamination and growth of pathogens.
Sanitizing
Reducing microorganisms on cleaned surfaces to an acceptable level using an approved method.
Shelf life
The period for which food remains suitable under specified storage conditions; it is not identical for every product.
Sterilization
A validated process intended to destroy or remove all viable microorganisms, including resistant forms, under defined conditions.
Temperature danger zone
A temperature range where many harmful bacteria can multiply quickly; exact limits depend on the applicable food-safety guidance.
Time-temperature control
Managing both how hot or cold food is and how long it stays there to reduce microbial risk.
Use-by date
A date that can carry a food-safety meaning for highly perishable food in some legal systems; follow local labeling rules.
Nutrition and diets24
Added sugar
Sugar introduced during processing or cooking rather than naturally present within an intact food.
Bioavailability
How much of a nutrient can be absorbed and used by the body from a food.
Calorie
A unit of energy; food labels usually use kilocalories, written as kcal or sometimes Calories.
Carbohydrate
A group of nutrients including sugars, starches, and fiber; some are used as a major energy source.
Complete protein
A protein source providing all essential amino acids in useful proportions.
Dietary fiber
Plant-derived carbohydrate material that is not fully digested in the small intestine and supports digestive function.
Dietary restriction
A rule about what a person avoids or limits for medical, religious, ethical, or personal reasons.
Energy density
The number of calories in a given weight of food; oil is more energy-dense than most vegetables.
Essential amino acid
An amino acid the body cannot make in sufficient amounts and must obtain from food.
Gluten-free diet
Eating without gluten-containing ingredients, essential for people with celiac disease and relevant to certain other conditions.
Glycemic index
A measure of how quickly a carbohydrate-containing food raises blood glucose under standardized testing.
Glycemic load
A measure combining a food's glycemic index with the amount of available carbohydrate in a serving.
Lactose intolerance
Difficulty digesting lactose, the sugar in milk, usually because the lactase enzyme is insufficient.
Macronutrient
A nutrient needed in relatively large amounts, commonly referring to protein, fat, and carbohydrate.
Micronutrient
A vitamin or mineral needed in small amounts to support normal body functions.
Nutrient density
How much useful nutrition a food supplies relative to its energy content or portion size.
Nutrition label
Information on a food package describing energy and selected nutrients, often per 100 g or per serving.
Saturated fat
Fats rich in fatty acids without double bonds; they occur in many animal fats and some tropical oils.
Serving suggestion
A presentation or eating idea on a package or in a recipe; it does not automatically define nutrient values.
Sodium
A mineral present in salt and many foods; nutrition labels often report sodium rather than total salt.
Trans fat
Fats containing trans-form unsaturated fatty acids; industrially produced trans fats should be minimized.
Unsaturated fat
Fats containing fatty acids with one or more double bonds; many vegetable oils contain them.
Vegan diet
An eating pattern that excludes animal-derived foods and ingredients.
Vegetarian diet
An eating pattern that avoids meat and fish; whether eggs and dairy are included depends on the type.
Cuisine and menu language19
Appetizer
A relatively small dish served before a main course to begin a meal.
Authenticity
How closely a dish reflects a recognized tradition; recipes can have multiple legitimate local versions.
Comfort food
A familiar dish associated with warmth, nostalgia, or emotional satisfaction.
Condiment
A sauce, seasoning, or prepared accompaniment added to food to adjust its flavor.
Course
One stage of a meal, such as a starter, main dish, or dessert.
Flavor profile
A simple description of dominant flavor characteristics, such as sweet, smoky, sour, and spicy.
Food pairing
Choosing foods or drinks that work well together in flavor, texture, or serving context.
Fusion cuisine
Cooking that intentionally combines elements from different culinary traditions.
Main course
The central dish of a meal, often its most substantial part.
Palate cleanser
A small bite or drink served between courses to refresh the mouth before the next flavors.
Plating style
The visual approach used to arrange food on a plate, from casual to minimalist.
Prix fixe
A set meal offered at a stated price, usually with defined courses or options.
Regional cuisine
Cooking traditions associated with a particular geographic area and its people.
Seasonal menu
Dishes planned around foods that are locally available or at their best during a season.
Side dish
Food served alongside the main dish, such as rice, vegetables, or salad.
Signature dish
A dish strongly associated with a particular restaurant, cook, or style.
Staple food
A food eaten frequently that provides a significant share of daily energy in a community.
Tasting menu
A sequence of small courses chosen to let guests sample several dishes or ideas.
À la carte
A menu format in which dishes are chosen individually rather than as a fixed set.
Kitchen operations and costing20
Allergen matrix
A table showing which allergens are present or may be involved in each dish, subject to careful verification.
As-purchased weight
The food's weight before preparation losses, often including peel, bones, or packaging exclusions.
Batch record
A record of a specific production run, such as recipe version, time, quantities, and checks.
Consistency of service
Providing similar portions, quality, and presentation every time a dish is ordered.
Edible portion
The quantity of food remaining for use after removing bones, shells, peels, or other unusable material.
Edible yield percentage
Edible portion weight divided by the as-purchased weight, multiplied by 100.
FEFO
First expired, first out: prioritize stock with the earliest applicable expiry date.
FIFO
First in, first out: use older suitable stock before newer stock when appropriate.
Food cost percentage
Ingredient cost divided by selling price, expressed as a percentage; other business costs still exist.
Food waste
Food discarded or lost during storage, preparation, service, or consumption.
Gross margin
The difference between sales revenue and the defined direct costs of goods sold, before other expenses.
Inventory
The foods and supplies currently held by a kitchen or food business.
Portion cost
The production cost assigned to one serving of a dish.
Prep list
A checklist of tasks that should be completed before service or a cooking session.
Production schedule
A timetable showing what must be prepared and when, so several dishes are ready on time.
Quality control
Checks that food meets defined expectations for weight, appearance, flavor, or safety-related conditions.
Recipe cost
The cost of the ingredients and other defined inputs needed to prepare the recipe.
Standardized recipe
A recipe with fixed ingredients, quantities, steps, and yield to make results more consistent.
Trim loss
The difference between purchased and usable food after unwanted parts are removed.
Waste log
A record of discarded food and reasons, useful for reducing avoidable waste.
