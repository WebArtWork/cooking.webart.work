Yogurt, Kefir, and Cultured Dairy

Theory 210 of 288

Core idea

Typical yogurt cultures include Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus. As they metabolize lactose, pH decreases; casein micelles destabilize and form the characteristic gel. Heating milk before also alters whey proteins, often improving and water retention.

Scientific / practical principles

A Utah State University tested process heats milk to 85–93 °C, cools to about 44–46 °C before adding starter, cultures for several hours until pH reaches 4.6 or lower, then refrigerates immediately. These values belong to that tested process; different dairy systems may use other validated schedules. Heating the milk does not make unclean utensils acceptable.

How to apply it

Use pasteurized milk, a food-grade starter, a reliable thermometer and clean vessels. Follow the heat, cool, culture and chill sequence from a tested recipe; avoid disturbing a set-style yogurt during incubation. Strain after chilling for a thicker Greek-style texture.

Practical example

Prepare yogurt with the same starter and milk, then compare the texture of an undisturbed sample with a sample stirred after setting. Taste only if both met the tested safety process.

Common mistakes and limitations

Incubating indefinitely to fix weak yogurt can create excessive without addressing the cause. Do not culture raw milk without validated controls, and never leave finished yogurt unrefrigerated.

Additional insight

Yogurt is commonly fermented by defined thermophilic bacterial starters, while kefir grains typically contain mixed lactic-acid bacteria and yeasts. They differ in gas production, , texture and incubation conditions; do not substitute their cultures into one another's safety protocol.
